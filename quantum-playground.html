Bit vs. Qubit
A classical bit is a light switch. A qubit is more like a spinning coin in the air.
0
0 and 1

A classical bit is always definitely 0 or definitely 1 — flip the switch and it snaps instantly.

A qubit doesn't have to pick a side right away. While it's spinning, it's genuinely both at once, each with its own chance of showing up when you finally check.

Classical = one state. Quantum = every possibility, together.

A classical computer checks doors one at a time — sometimes it gets unlucky and checks the wrong two first.

A quantum computer lets a qubit explore all three doors together, then collapses onto the right one when measured.

Exploring possibilities together is why some quantum searches are faster.
03
Measurement: the coin collapses
Spin it, then look. The instant you look, chance turns into a fact.
?
Spin it first

While the coin is spinning in the air, asking "heads or tails?" doesn't really have an answer yet — it's both, blended together.

The moment it lands (or you measure a qubit), it has to commit to one outcome. That's called collapse, and you can't predict exactly which side you'll get — only how likely each one is.

Before measuring: a blend of possibilities. After: one definite answer.
04
Entanglement: linked qubits
Two qubits can become a matched pair — check one, and you instantly know the other.
A
Click orb A

These two qubits were prepared as a linked pair. Click orb A to measure it — notice orb B changes to match, instantly, without anyone touching it.

They're not sending each other a signal. They were correlated from the start — measuring one just reveals what the other must be.

Entangled qubits share an outcome, even far apart.

Add gates, then press ▶

H (a "spreading" gate) pushes the qubit into superposition — 0 and 1 at once.
X flips it, like a NOT.

Click H, then X, then hit ▶ to watch the qubit move through each step, gate by gate — just like a real quantum circuit, only smaller.

Quantum programs are sequences of gates that reshape a qubit's possibilities.
